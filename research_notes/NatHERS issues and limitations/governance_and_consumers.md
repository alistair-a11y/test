## 1. Governance: who decides, how it is funded, and what the 2026 rebrand and Stage 2 changed

### Takeaway
NatHERS is run by committees of governments:
- **Energy and climate ministers (ECMC)** set the direction.
- **A Steering Committee** of Commonwealth, state and territory officials oversees the scheme.
- **DCCEEW, as Administrator,** runs it day to day and reports to that committee.
- **Industry** has input only through advisory groups with no executive power.
- **States** decide adoption, enforcement and disclosure.

Apart from the $32.7 million existing-homes extension (March 2026), funding is not transparently published.

Stage 2 (mid-2026) launched a consumer-facing "Home Energy Rating" brand and website for buyers and renters, tied to green finance and state disclosure. This pushes NatHERS from a new-home compliance tool toward the common energy label for the whole housing stock. Existing-home ratings cannot be used for NCC compliance.

#### G1. Layered intergovernmental governance, with non-binding industry advice
- **What it is:** Decision rights are split between ministers, a Steering Committee of officials, the Administrator and the states. The advisory bodies (the Stakeholder Consultative Group, SCG, and the Technical Advisory Committee, TAC) have no executive power.

## 2. Money flows: who earns what, who benefits, and conflict-of-interest rules

### Takeaway
The money in NatHERS flows through published fee schedules, not hidden payments:
- **Software vendors** charge per-certificate fees that include a CSIRO royalty. One vendor is the Victorian Government.
- **AAOs** collect membership and accreditation fees while also quality-assuring their own members.
- **Assessors** are paid by builders or owners.

At about 178,000 certificates a year, software and royalty fees are a modest single-digit-million-dollar stream (my estimate).

For new homes, conflicts of interest are handled by disclosure (a "Declaration of interest" on the certificate, plus AAO declarations), not prohibition. NatHERS's own sample certificate shows an assessor "Employed by designer of the building". The 2025 existing-homes rules are stricter. No documented case of builder-owned assessors inflating ratings was found, and no evidence of impropriety.

### Cited Findings

#### M1. The per-certificate fee chain: software vendors, CSIRO royalty, a state-owned vendor
- **What it is:** Every regulatory certificate carries a software fee. Established: about $27–45 + GST each, including about $13 CSIRO royalty.
- **New evidence (volumes):**
  - "since 2016 ... the Scheme has rated over 1.3 million residential dwellings and in the last year alone more than 178 thousand certificates have been issued" (government, Dec 2023; search extract) — [NatHERS Star, 19 Dec 2023](https://nathers.gov.au/node/569)
  - CSIRO data: 216,939 certificates from Sep 2017 to Sep 2018, across building classes (search extract; attribution to this newsletter uncertain) — [NatHERS newsletter, Dec 2018](https://nathers.gov.au/node/319)
- **Counter-evidence (fees are small relative to compliance costs):**
  - Perth's average upgrade to 7 stars is estimated at $4,262, against $4,311 across the largest cities (consultant Graham Energy for the Australian Glass and Window Association, an industry body; search extract) — [National Tribune](https://www.nationaltribune.com.au/home-building-costs-perth-projections-beat-national-average-for-7-star-energy-compliance/)
  - RMIT researchers estimated an average capital cost increase of about A$1,704 for detached houses and townhouses (search extract) — [The Conversation via One Step Off The Grid](https://onestepoffthegrid.com.au/looking-lower-energy-bills-new-build-homes-dont-always-deliver/amp)

#### M2. AAO fees, and the AAOs' dual role as member bodies and quality assurers
- **What it is:** AAOs are membership organisations that charge assessors fees and are also the first line of quality assurance (QA) on those same members.
- **Evidence:**
  - **Design Matters National membership page (industry; current):** Full membership "$47.30/month" billed annually ($52.03 billed monthly). Individual membership "$24.50/month" billed annually ($26.95 billed monthly), which is "required for Existing Homes Accreditation applicants".   - **ABSA in 2010 (historical; government Senate Estimates answer):** membership $220, accreditation $660, "scheme fees" $440. Total applicant costs were about $3,463–$4,063 including insurance, training and police checks (search extract) — [Senate Estimates CC84, 2010](https://aph.gov.au/~/media/Estimates/Live/fapa_ctte/estimates/add_0910/climate_change/CC84.ashx)
  - **QA delegated to AAOs (regulator, 2022):** "Each year, AAOs undertake a Quality Assurance review of 20 per cent of their Accredited Assessors under a process agreed with the NatHERS Administrator" (search extract) — [VBA](https://www.vba.vic.gov.au/news/news/2022/clarification-nathers-for-alterations-and-additions)
  - **Government, Nov 2024:** The Administrator is "developing a policy to help Assessor Accrediting Organisations manage quality assurance more consistently" (search extract) — [NatHERS News, 13 Nov 2024](https://nathers.gov.au/node/588). "The current version of the AAO Protocol was released in November 2024" (v2.2; search extract) — [AAO Protocol v2.2](https://nathers.gov.au/sites/default/files/2024-11/AAOProtocol%20v2.2.pdf)
- **Counter-evidence:**
  - AAOs operate under an Administrator-approved protocol (above).
  - For existing homes, "Independent Auditors provide independent audits using standardised, risk-based processes that have been developed by the NatHERS Administrator" (search extract) — [Existing Homes Assurance Strategy 2025](https://www.nathers.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-07/NatHERS%20for%20Existing%20Homes%20Assurance%20Strategy.pdf)

#### M3. For new homes, conflicts are disclosed, not prohibited
- **What it is:** For new homes, an assessor linked to the designer or applicant may rate the home, provided the link is declared on the certificate. (The established note covers AAO Declaration of Interest forms.)
- **Evidence:**
  - **NatHERS guide to pre-2019 certificates (government):** The assessor details include a "Declaration of interest" field, with the sample entry "Employed by designer of the building" (search extract) — [NatHERS guide to old certificates](https://www.nathers.gov.au/sites/default/files/2022-09/Guides%20to%20the%20Old%20%28pre%202019%29%20universal%20certificates.pdf)
  - **Real certificates lodged with NSW applications** show entries such as "None" and "The Assessor has provided design advice to the Applicant" (search extract; which document carries which entry is uncertain) — [Beane St NatHERS certificate, NSW Planning Portal](https://www.planningportal.nsw.gov.au/sites/default/files/documents/2022/Beane%20St%20NatHERS%20Certificate%20-%20Gosford.PDF); [NSW Planning Portal document PEH-709](https://apps.planningportal.nsw.gov.au/prweb/PRRestService/DocMgmt/v1/PublicDocuments/DATA-WORKATTACH-FILE%20PEC-DPE-EP-WORK%20PEH-709!20210901T040554.683%20GMT)
  - **NatHERS 2022 template** keeps the field: "Declaration of interest [declaration]" (search extract) — [NatHERS expansion infographic](https://www.nathers.gov.au/sites/default/files/2022-08/Infographic%20-%20NatHERS%20expansion_0.pdf)
  - **Existing homes, a stricter regime (government, Jul 2025):**
    - Conflict of Interest Policy: a conflict is "a circumstance where an assessor holds a personal or business interest that may influence, or be perceived by a reasonable person to influence, any part of a NatHERS assessment". Assessors declare interests at accreditation and annually (search extract) — [Existing Homes Conflict of Interest Policy](https://www.nathers.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-07/NatHERS%20for%20existing%20homes%20Assessor%20Conflict%20of%20Interest%20Policy.pdf)
    - The 2024 draft Code barred generating certificates "for any property in which they hold a financial interest" and required conflicts to be declared to homeowners or tenants (search extract; final wording unconfirmed) — [Draft Code of Practice 2024](https://nathers.gov.au/sites/default/files/2024-08/Consultation%20Draft%20-%20NatHERS%20for%20existing%20homes%20Assessor%20Code%20of%20Practice.pdf)
    - The final Code requires assessors to report changes that "could reasonably be considered to give rise to an actual or perceived conflict of interest" (search extract) — [Code of Practice 2025](https://www.nathers.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-07/NatHERS%20for%20existing%20homes%20Assessor%20Code%20of%20Practice.pdf)
  - **Builders employing or owning their assessors:** Two targeted searches found no documented case and no regulator finding. The nearest analogue concerns building certifiers, not NatHERS assessors: a 2019 NSW parliamentary submission argued that certifiers "employed by the builder/developer have an inherent conflict of interest" (search extract; indirect) — [NSW Parliament submission](https://www.parliament.nsw.gov.au/lcdocs/submissions/64265/0088%20Name%20Suppressed.pdf)
- **Counter-evidence:**
  - The declaration is printed on the certificate, where certifiers and owners can see it.
  - In NSW, BASIX certifiers check the accredited assessor's details — [NSW Planning Portal, accredited assessor details](https://www.planningportal.nsw.gov.au/basix-thermal-comfort/simulation-method/certifying-thermal-comfort/accredited-assessor-details)

  - Brokers see an opportunity to add value — [MPA magazine](https://www.mpamag.com/au/news/general/home-energy-rating-expansion-seen-as-chance-for-brokers-to-add-value/581208)

- **Evidence:**

  - **DMN** is both a new-home AAO and the exclusive existing-homes accreditation provider — [DMN](https://designmatters.org.au/Web/News/Articles/2026/NatHERS-expansion-milestone-DMN-to-accredit-NatHERS-for-Existing-Homes-Assessors.aspx)

## 3. Accreditation and policing: state mandates, training, audits, sanctions and criticism

### Takeaway
Accreditation is mandatory for regulatory ratings only in some places:
- **NSW (BASIX, verified):** a NatHERS-accredited assessor is required.
- **ACT:** licensed assessors are required.
- **Elsewhere:** NatHERS itself says some jurisdictions allow non-accredited "raters", who need no qualifications, insurance, CPD or QA.
- **WA:** one practitioner estimated that around 80% of homes in 2016–18 went through the VURB pathway or non-accredited assessors.

Accredited assessors need a Certificate IV. Current numbers are not published; the last figure found is "over 600" in 2017–18. QA is delegated to AAOs, which reviewed about 20% of assessors a year as of 2022. Sanctions go up to loss of accreditation, but outcomes are not published.

Documented weaknesses:
- inconsistent ratings between assessors (acknowledged by the WA Building Commission);
- widespread non-compliance (NEEBP, 2014);
- suspected fraudulent certificates (2018);
- no routine as-built verification.

No Auditor-General or ANAO audit of NatHERS was found. **Most of these criticisms apply equally or more to H6V2.**

## 4. Consumer understanding, builder marketing and disclosure

### Takeaway
No published study was found that directly measures whether new-home buyers understand NatHERS stars. The indirect evidence points to poor understanding:
- **Builder marketing:** a 2020 peer-reviewed study found volume builders presenting the 6-star minimum as "superior", as "sustainability", or as a complete set of six stars.
- **Homeowner perception:** a DCCEEW trial reports that homeowners "significantly overestimate" their homes' performance.

Where ratings are shown, they matter. Disclosure shifts choices in government experiments, and ACT ratings carry price premiums.

Yet roughly 93–97% of listings show no rating. Only the ACT mandates disclosure. The national Disclosure Framework is a non-binding template for states. NSW planned voluntary disclosure from mid-2026, with a mandatory scheme to follow at a date not yet set.

### Cited Findings

#### C1. Misleading star marketing by volume builders (peer-reviewed, 2020)
- **Evidence:**
  - **The study:** Warren-Myers, Bartak and Cradduck (author list per search extract), "Observing energy rating stars through the Australian Consumer Law lens: How volume home builders' advertising can fail consumers", *Energy Policy*, 2020 (peer-reviewed) — [University of Melbourne record](https://findanexpert.unimelb.edu.au/scholarlywork/1440488-observing-energy-rating-stars-through-the-australian-consumer-law-lens--how-volume-home-builders'-advertising-can-fail-consumers)
  - **Findings, from the study and its companion article (search extracts):**
    - Builders used "logos showing a grouping of six stars only, suggesting six is the best rating, claiming six-star 'sustainability' and even offering this minimum standard 'at no extra cost'".
    - One site called six stars "superior".
    - Such conduct could engage section 18(1) of the Australian Consumer Law, which prohibits misleading or deceptive conduct.
    - "While builders may not intend to mislead buyers, this may be an outcome of current advertising practices."
    - "Most new home buyers are not well versed in building design or energy efficiency regulations" and rely on builders.
    - None of the sites examined used the official NatHERS logo.
  - **Sources for the findings:** [Spruiking the stars (UniMelb)](https://findanexpert.unimelb.edu.au/news/12072-spruiking-the-stars--some-home-builders-are-misleading-consumers-about-energy-ratings); [CSIRO AHD repost](https://ahd.csiro.au/?p=1249); [One Step Off The Grid](https://onestepoffthegrid.com.au/how-some-home-builders-are-misleading-consumers-about-energy-ratings/)
- **Counter-evidence:**
  - It is a content analysis of websites, not proof of consumer harm, and the authors say intent was not shown.
  - The sample predates the 7-star minimum.
  - No regulator action was found, though ACCC action was not specifically searched.

#### C2. Confusion over the star scale and what it covers
- **Evidence:**
  - "The ten-star NatHERS scale differs from many other commonly used star rating schemes, such as Green Star, where five or six stars indicate best, not minimum, performance" (search extract) — [Spruiking the stars](https://findanexpert.unimelb.edu.au/news/12072-spruiking-the-stars--some-home-builders-are-misleading-consumers-about-energy-ratings)
  - On appliance labels, "most products are given between 1 and 6 stars" (government; search extract) — [Energy Rating label](https://energyrating.gov.au/label)
  - The NatHERS star estimates thermal performance (heating and cooling needs). Appliance energy was added through the separate Whole-of-Home expansion — [NatHERS Whole of Home](https://www.nathers.gov.au/node/484)
  - The existing-home Home Energy Rating adds appliances and solar plus a score out of 100 — [node/636](https://www.nathers.gov.au/node/636)
- **Counter-evidence:** The new brand adds plain-language resources for buyers and renters — [pv magazine](https://www.pv-magazine-australia.com/?p=17014)

#### C3. Homeowners overestimate their homes' performance
- **Evidence:** DCCEEW Home Energy Rating Live Trial, as described by its research agency Instinct and Reason (consultant; date not in extract): "most NSW homes rate just 1.8 stars out of 10 for energy efficiency, yet homeowners significantly overestimate their performance" (search extract) — [Instinct and Reason case study](https://instinctandreason.com/case-studies/dcceew-home-energy-rating-live-trial/)
- **Counter-evidence and context:** The Behavioural Economics Team of the Australian Government (BETA, in PM&C), June 2025 (government):
  - Survey experiments with 13,797 Australians found: "Mandatory disclosure of Home Energy ratings helped participants choose to inspect homes with higher energy ratings, and helped sellers prioritise energy upgrades over cosmetic upgrades" (search extract).
  - These were stated choices in hypothetical scenarios.
  - Sources: [BETA report](https://www.pmc.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-06/report-home-energy-upgrades.pdf); [BETA summary](https://www.pmc.gov.au/news/empowering-homeowners-make-informed-decisions)

#### C4. Ratings are missing from most listings
- **Evidence:**
  - **Cotality and the Real Estate Institute of Australia (REIA), *Efficiency Edge* report** (industry; date not in extract, likely 2025–26):
    - "almost 97 per cent of Australian property listings do not currently disclose a home energy rating" (search extract).
    - Government behavioural research found 86% of participants consider it important to see a rating when buying and 75% when renting (search extract).
    - Sources: [Money magazine](https://www.moneymag.com.au/missing-home-energy-ratings-property-listings); [Elite Agent](https://eliteagent.com/home-energy-ratings-new-value-lever-for-agents/)
  - **PropTrack / REA Group** (industry; date not in extract):
    - 7% of realestate.com.au listings nationally declare a rating.
    - "Only 15% of Victorian listings" do, second to the ACT, where disclosure is "virtually mandatory".
    - 56% of property seekers rate energy-efficiency ratings "extremely important" (search extracts).
    - Sources: [The Property Tribune](https://thepropertytribune.com.au/lifestyle/more-australians-seeking-energy-efficient-homes); [Broker News](https://www.brokernews.com.au/news/breaking-news/property-seekers-on-the-lookout-for-energyefficient-homes-as-energy-bills-rise-281091.aspx)
- **Counter-evidence:**
  - The 97% and 7% figures come from different datasets and years.
  - NSW's voluntary rollout from mid-2026 may lift disclosure — [Energy NSW](https://www.energy.nsw.gov.au/households/action/initiatives/home-energy-ratings)

#### C5. The ACT's experience of mandatory disclosure
- **Evidence:**
  - "The ACT is the only Australian State or Territory to introduce mandatory rating disclosure for all dwellings" (search extract).
  - **Warren-Myers and Fuerst** (ACT sales 2011–2016; peer-reviewed research; university release 2018):
    - 5- and 6-star homes sold for 2% and 2.4% more than 3-star homes; 7-star homes for "up to 9.4" per cent more.
    - The relationship "is not linear, with premiums dropping for an 8+ star home".

## 5. What the scheme admits needs fixing: plans, reviews and planned reforms

### Takeaway
The last strategic plan found covers 2018–2022 and was built on an independent governance and operational review. No successor was located. Since then, the scheme's admissions show up as reforms:
- Whole-of-Home (conceding the star covered only the shell).
- A Compliance and Enforcement Framework (April 2024).
- AAO Protocol v2.2 and a policy to make AAO quality assurance consistent (November 2024).
- A full set of assurance, conflict-of-interest and CPD rules for existing homes (2025).
- New workforce pathways (November 2025) to address "very low" assessor supply.
- Ministers' agreement to update the Trajectory.

Mandatory disclosure remains an aim everywhere except the ACT.

### Cited Findings

#### R1. No current strategic plan located
- **Evidence:**
  - The *NatHERS Strategic Plan 2018–2022* was built around four strategic goals drawn from "an independent governance and operational review". Its priorities included simplifying processes, strengthening links with building energy policy, and supporting other residential building initiatives (government; search extract) — [Strategic Plan 2018–2022](https://www.nathers.gov.au/sites/default/files/NathersStrategicPlan-2018-2022.pdf)
