# Real-world evidence on NatHERS-rated new homes: modelled vs measured performance, rating accuracy and the as-built gap

**Scope.** Australia; NatHERS (publicly branded "Home Energy Rating" since 1 July 2026) for new homes, mainly Class 1 houses, bringing in Western Australia (WA) where evidence exists. Current to 22 October 2026.

**Method.** I tried WebFetch once and it failed (`getaddrinfo ENOTFOUND ahd.csiro.au`), so no source page was opened.

- **Every quotation below is a search extract.** It is the search tool's excerpt, which may be lightly paraphrased. Check it against the original before publishing.
- 45 web searches were used.
- Items already established in Thermarate's strategy doc are not repeated. The exception is where I add a primary source, extra detail or a correction; those are flagged **Addition** or **Correction**.

**Rating keys** (both ratings are my assessments):

- **Strength.**
  - *Strong:* several independent field or peer-reviewed studies agree.
  - *Moderate:* one solid study, or several weaker studies that agree.
  - *Weak:* a small sample, anecdote, industry or advocacy claim, or second-hand reporting only.
- **H6V2 backfire:** whether the same criticism would also hit a simulation-based H6V2 Performance Solution.
- **Marketing:** use / use with care / avoid, plus a suggested accurate one-line wording.

**Background for the H6V2 judgements.** Thermarate's strategy doc records three things:

- H6V2 "runs the same kind of simulation, with fixed thermostat settings, schedules and occupancy".
- The ABCB barred NatHERS software from the reference-building method in 2019.
- The ABCB also fixed that method's schedules and thermostat settings.

Sources: [strategy doc](../../Performance_Solutions_awareness_strategy.md); [NatHERS: Use of NatHERS tools for the NCC Reference Building Verification Method](https://nathers.gov.au/node/125); [FirstRate5 news, Aug 2017: NatHERS statement on the NCC reference building verification method](https://www.fr5.com.au/news/2017/08/07/nathers-statement-on-national-construction-code-reference-building-verification-method).

No study was found of how H6V2 Performance Solutions perform once built, or how consistent they are between modellers.

## 1. Modelled vs measured: do NatHERS ratings predict real energy use and comfort?

### Takeaway
**On average, higher-rated homes use less heating energy, but individual homes vary widely.** Summer and cooling results are mixed, or worse for higher-rated homes in temperate cities. Real thermostat behaviour departs from the rating assumptions in both directions. The decisive large-scale test, CSIRO linking its 1.6 million-plus ratings to meter data, is still under way, with no results found by 22 October 2026. Almost every limitation here comes from standardised simulation assumptions, and an H6V2 model shares them.

### Cited Findings

#### 1.1 The big test is pending: CSIRO's meter-data linkage for its 1.6 million ratings
- **What it is.** NatHERS has never been checked at national scale against metered energy use. CSIRO has started that work, but no findings had been published by October 2026.
- **Evidence.**
  - **Meter-data linkage.** CSIRO says it is "working to gain access to electricity and gas meter data to compare against its database of over 1.6 million NatHERS ratings". The aim is to test, in aggregate, how well ratings track real consumption.
    - Source: CSIRO Australian Housing Data (AHD), *NatHERS tools update December 2025* (December 2025; government research agency; search extract) — [AHD, Dec 2025](https://ahd.csiro.au/nationwide-house-energy-rating-scheme-nathers-tools-update-december-2025/)
    - The same update warns "a very large sample is required to smooth out individual occupant behaviours" (search extract) — [AHD, Dec 2025](https://ahd.csiro.au/nationwide-house-energy-rating-scheme-nathers-tools-update-december-2025/)
  - **Apartment Energy Behaviour Study** (Class 2, so indirect for houses).
    - Scope: 430 apartments in Melbourne, Sydney, Brisbane and Darwin, monitored for indoor temperatures, window opening, CO₂, thermostat use and appliance use.
    - Status: the first year of monitoring ended in December 2025, "with findings to be reviewed by the NatHERS Administrator" (search extract) — [AHD, Dec 2025](https://ahd.csiro.au/nationwide-house-energy-rating-scheme-nathers-tools-update-december-2025/)
    - Recruitment (2024) was framed as a study that "will help to test assumptions in the Nationwide House Energy Rating Scheme" (search extract) — [CSIRO Smart Energy newsletter, Oct 2024](https://www.csiro.au/en/Newsletters/National-Energy-Analysis-Centre/2024-10); [SolarQuotes](https://www.solarquotes.com.au/blog/apartment-energy-study-mb3117/)
  - **Scheme reach.** There were 96.5 NatHERS certificates per 100 building permits in 2025, up from about 80 in 2018 (search extract) — [AHD, Dec 2025](https://ahd.csiro.au/nationwide-house-energy-rating-scheme-nathers-tools-update-december-2025/)
- **Strength.** Strong that the results are not yet public: CSIRO's own statement says so, and two targeted searches found no publication. There is no evidence yet either way on what the results will show.
- **Counter-evidence.** CSIRO's earlier household study reportedly found a strong correlation once occupants were accounted for.
  - The study: 209 households in Melbourne, Brisbane and Adelaide, houses built 2000–2010, recruited 2012/13, with half-hourly indoor temperatures and the NatHERS rating recorded — [AHD 209-household study](https://ahd.csiro.au/?p=889)
  - The finding: predicted and actual energy "showed a strong correlation when different occupants were accounted for" (search extract; no coefficients seen; the extract does not say which of these two pages it came from) — [CSIRO, "It's in the stars!" (2021)](https://www.csiro.au/en/news/all/articles/2021/september/its-in-the-stars-how-scientists-figure-out-your-homes-energy-rating); [AHD, Typical house energy use](https://ahd.csiro.au/other-data/typical-house-energy-use/)
- **H6V2 backfire.** High. No meter-data validation of H6V2 reference-building comparisons was found at all. Saying "NatHERS has never been validated" invites the reply "neither has yours".
- **Marketing.** **Avoid** as an attack. If it is used at all, *use with care*:
  - "CSIRO is still collecting meter data to test how well ratings track real bills, so treat any energy model, ours included, as an estimate."

#### 1.2 Same rating, very different bills: occupants swamp the rating for any single home
- **What it is.** At the level of one home, measured heating and cooling energy varies widely within the same star band, mostly because of occupant behaviour.
- **Evidence.**
  - **Perth, 10 homes** (WA).
    - Source: CRC for Low Carbon Living (CRC LCL) project RP3009 student poster, 2015 (academic, early-stage; search extract) — [Eon poster RP3009](https://www.lowcarbonlivingcrc.unsw.edu.au/sites/all/files/publications_file_attachments/25_christine_eon_student_poster_2015_rp3009.pdf)
    - "House G (7-Stars), for example, used more energy per occupant than two of the 6-Star homes."
    - Consumption "varied significantly between houses rated within the same NatHERS Star band".
    - "House M sets the heater at 26oC, while Houses L and O set it at 21oC. House P in contrast does not seem to heat the house during this period."
  - **The Conversation**, republished; date not confirmed, around 2017 per the search summary (media article by academics; search extract) — [One Step Off The Grid republication](https://onestepoffthegrid.com.au/looking-lower-energy-bills-new-build-homes-dont-always-deliver/)
    - "a study of 10 homes in Perth found significant variation in energy use between homes with the same rating".
  - **Lochiel Park vs Mawson Lakes, Adelaide** (O'Leary et al. 2016). *Addition to the established item.*
    - Cooling use was "closely correlated with predictions".
    - Heating was less consistent, "with some homes using two to three times the NatHERS predicted energy use".
    - The researcher suggested occupant behaviour or leaky homes as the cause (search extract) — [The Fifth Estate](https://thefifthestate.com.au/?p=81654)
  - **NSW BASIX measured validation.**
    - Source: UNSW / CRC LCL final report; date not confirmed, probably 2017–19 (government-funded academic; search extract) — [GBPN listing](https://library.gbpn.org/resource/validating-and-improving-basix-energy-assessment-tool-low-carbon-dwellings-final-report); [UNSW project page](https://www.be.unsw.edu.au/research/research-activities/high-performance-architecture-research-cluster/projects/validating-0)
    - Measured dwellings "were close to BASIX estimations, but the breakdown of energy consumption highlighted discrepancies for space heating and cooling, lighting and plug loads".
  - **CSIRO, in a Productivity Commission submission** (around 2005; government; search extract) — [PC submission 69](https://www.pc.gov.au/inquiries/completed/energy-efficiency/submissions/department_of_the_environment_and_heritage/sub069.pdf)
    - The tool "measures the inherent thermal performance of the building shell all other things being equal".
    - Correlation with energy use rises "markedly once energy performance of appliances is adjusted for".
  - **Rammed-earth houses** (IBPSA Building Simulation 2019; peer-reviewed conference; search extract) — [IBPSA BS2019](https://publications.ibpsa.org/proceedings/bs/2019/papers/BS2019_210313.pdf)
    - Found a gap between measured energy use and NatHERS ratings.
    - "With the average house in Australia lasting around 60 years, those thermostat assumptions could significantly affect residential energy use and emissions."
  - **Older Adelaide study** (University of Adelaide repository; year not confirmed). It reportedly tested the assumption that higher-rated homes use less energy and found it wanting against measured heating and cooling. This is the search tool's summary, not a quote — [hdl 2440/29014](https://web4.library.adelaide.edu.au/dspace/bitstream/2440/29014/1/hdl_29014.pdf)
- **Strength.** Strong that individual homes diverge; the finding is consistent across Perth, Adelaide, NSW and CSIRO's own framing. Weak for any claim that ratings fail to predict on average (see Section 5).
- **Counter-evidence.**
  - In aggregate, higher stars use less heating and cooling energy (O'Leary 2016, established; Section 5).
  - CSIRO's 209-household study reports a "strong correlation" once occupants are accounted for (1.1).
  - In NSW, BASIX-rated homes landed close to their estimates in aggregate.
- **H6V2 backfire.** High. This is the same issue, because H6V2 uses fixed occupancy, schedules and thermostats.
- **Marketing.** *Use with care.* It is pathway-neutral and consistent with NatHERS's own caveat that actual use "may vary" (see strategy doc):
  - "A rating models the building, not your household. Homes with the same stars can have very different bills."

#### 1.3 Summer: better-rated homes in temperate cities can run warmer and use more cooling energy
- **What it is.** In mild climates, more insulation without matching shading and ventilation can hold heat in summer. A single annual star figure hides this.
- **Evidence.**
  - **Willand, Ridley & Pears (2016), *Energy and Buildings*** (peer-reviewed; 107 Melbourne homes; search extract) — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0378778815304692); [CityU record](https://scholars.cityu.edu.hk/en/publications/relationship-of-thermal-performance-rating-summer-indoor-temperat/)
    - Temperature: "at the heat wave threshold of 25 °C, on average, better rated 6-Star homes were 0.89 °C warmer than 4- or 5-Star rated homes".
    - Cooling energy: "Air-conditioned 6-Star homes needed 15.84 kWh/day electric cooling energy more than 3-Star homes to reach the same living room temperature". Check the units in the paper.
    - Conclusion: "increased fabric insulation may be associated with increased summer indoor temperatures, risk of heat stress and cooling energy in a mild temperate climate".
    - The authors recommend rating each season independently and promoting natural ventilation and shading.
  - **CSIRO 2013 evaluation.** *Addition to the established item* (higher cooling energy in higher-rated Brisbane and Melbourne houses): over a full year, greenhouse emissions were "still reduced by 7% for the higher-rated houses". The report also notes that occupant behaviour, such as window opening, affects results (search extract) — [DCCEEW-hosted report](https://www.dcceew.gov.au/sites/default/files/documents/evaluation-5-star-energy-efficiency-standard-residential-buildings-report.pdf)
- **Strength.** Moderate. Two measured datasets in temperate cities agree, but both are cross-sectional and confounded by air-conditioner ownership and occupants.
- **Counter-evidence.**
  - At Lochiel Park, cooling closely matched predictions (1.2).
  - CSIRO 2013 found a net 7% annual emissions reduction.
  - Against very poor older stock, higher ratings cut heat stress (4.4).
- **H6V2 backfire.** Moderate to high. H6V2 also optimises annual heating and cooling loads with conditioning assumed, so on its own it does not address free-running summer comfort. It becomes an advantage only if Thermarate adds a summer comfort check.
- **Marketing.** *Use with care.*
  - "A Melbourne study found better-insulated homes ran slightly warmer in summer. Shading and ventilation matter as much as stars."
  - Avoid: "7-star homes are hotter."

#### 1.4 Real thermostat behaviour differs from the rating's assumptions, and the direction depends on climate and on how a set-point is defined
- **What it is.** Ratings assume standard heating and cooling set-points and trigger temperatures. Monitored households differ, sometimes in opposite directions.
- **Evidence.**
  - **Temperate monitoring.**
    - Source: CRC LCL RP1024 (UNSW/CSIRO); 129 houses in Adelaide, Brisbane and Melbourne monitored 2012–2014 (research report; search extract) — [RP1024](https://www.unsw.edu.au/content/dam/pdfs/ada/built-environment/low-carbon-living-crc/resources/rp1024_indoor_air_temperatures.pdf)
    - "Cooling switch on temperature are around 0.5°C lower than the currently assumed values for the three cities."
    - "However, the median indoor temperatures, when A/C is running which may be considered as the real thermostat set point, are about 1.5°C above the currently assumed."
  - **Hot-dry NCC Climate Zone 4, South Australia** (2025, *Energy and Buildings*; peer-reviewed; search extract) — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0378778825014987)
    - "Trigger point temperatures were also found to be lower than NatHERS' assumed trigger point temperature."
    - "These results indicate that houses in this climate zone that have been rated by NatHERS could use more cooling energy than predicted."
    - The authors suggest the findings inform NatHERS occupant settings and NCC overheating checks.
  - **National household survey** (2026, *Energy Research & Social Science*; peer-reviewed; self-reported; search extract) — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214629626002823)
    - "Most households selected setpoints at or below 22 °C, while moderate settings (23–24 °C) were less common and higher settings (≥25 °C) were used by only a small minority."
  - **AccuRate temperature-settings study.**
    - Source: DCCEE-era government research report; date and sample size not confirmed; small Adelaide sample (search extract) — [homeenergyrating.gov.au](https://homeenergyrating.gov.au/sites/default/files/Research%20Rep%20-%20Accurate%20Temp%20Settings.pdf)
    - "Using the 'Default' settings from the previous report, the respective differences were -46.3 % and -33.1 %." The search summary reads this as default settings under-predicting measured cooling. Adjusted settings were proposed.
  - **Darwin, tropical** (2022; James Cook University; 38 houses rated 4–7 stars, 69 occupants; search extract) — [JCU](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/73717/)
    - "many people were comfortable in warmer and more humid conditions than is assumed by national building codes, with many people opting for use of fans".
- **Strength.** Moderate to strong that the assumptions do not match real households. The direction is inconsistent:
  - lower trigger points and set-points in hot-dry South Australia and in the survey;
  - a higher running temperature in temperate monitored homes;
  - higher tolerance of heat in Darwin.
- **Counter-evidence.** Standard settings are deliberate: they let buildings be compared, and were never meant to predict bills (CSIRO's statement in 1.2).
- **H6V2 backfire.** High, because H6V2 also uses prescribed thermostat settings.
- **Marketing.** *Use with care*, and only as a pathway-neutral point:
  - "Every compliance model, NatHERS or Performance Solution, assumes standard thermostat settings. Real households differ, so a predicted energy figure is a comparison, not a bill."

#### 1.5 Rebound and comfort-taking
- **What it is.** Part of an efficiency gain is taken as extra comfort, or extra use, instead of lower energy use.
- **Evidence.**
  - **"On the rebound: estimating direct rebound effects for Australian households"** (*Australian Journal of Agricultural and Resource Economics*; peer-reviewed econometrics; year not confirmed; covers all household energy, not NatHERS; search extract) — [AgEcon PDF](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/313562/files/ajar12230.pdf)
    - "the rebound effect is relatively high for energy use by Australian households. We find that low‐income households and households with vulnerable members have the largest rebound effects."
  - **Perth "takeback"** (The Conversation, around 2017): "some people in energy-efficient homes actually increase their energy consumption" (search extract) — [One Step Off The Grid](https://onestepoffthegrid.com.au/looking-lower-energy-bills-new-build-homes-dont-always-deliver/)
  - **Solar PV rebound** (CRC LCL; Ausgrid billing data 2007–2014): rebound "erodes up to one fifth of the benefit" of PV generation (search extract) — [CRC LCL RP2016](https://www.lowcarbonlivingcrc.unsw.edu.au/sites/all/files/publications_file_attachments/rp2016_assessing_impact_of_solar_pv.pdf)
- **Strength.** Weak for NatHERS-rated new homes specifically; no direct study was found. Moderate for Australian households in general.
- **Counter-evidence.**
  - **Victorian Healthy Homes Program** (Sustainability Victoria; retrofit trial with a control group; search extract) — [SV](https://www.sustainability.vic.gov.au/research-data-and-insights/research/research-reports/the-victorian-healthy-homes-program-research-findings)
    - Upgraded households were "more than twice as likely ... to report that their home felt warmer", "despite a significant reduction in gas use".
    - "There was no evidence of a rebound effect."
  - **UK government comfort-taking review** (non-Australian; date not confirmed): comfort-taking can be "a positive and desirable outcome" (search extract) — [UK review](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/69b142041daa1b70ca233159/comfort-taking-evidence-review.pdf)
- **H6V2 backfire.** High. Occupant response does not depend on the compliance pathway.
- **Marketing.** **Avoid** "efficient homes make people use more energy". *Use with care*:
  - "Part of an efficient home's benefit is often taken as extra comfort rather than a smaller bill. That's not a failure."

#### 1.6 Simulation engine accuracy: good on energy flows, weaker on indoor temperatures
- **What it is.** Tests of the CSIRO Chenath/AccuRate engine itself, separate from occupants and construction.
- **Evidence.**
  - **BESTEST validation of the AccuRate engine** (NatHERS-hosted technical report; date not confirmed; search extract) — [NatHERS PDF](https://www.nathers.gov.au/sites/default/files/Validation%2520of%2520the%2520AccuRate%2520Simulation%2520Engine%2520Using%2520BESTEST_0.pdf)
    - The engine "agrees very well with the reference programs, and no major discrepancies were found".
    - Because it controls environmental temperature rather than air temperature, "it tends to predict somewhat high cooling energies and peak heating and cooling demands".
  - **Daniel, Soebarto & Williamson (2012)** (University of Adelaide). Chenath V2.26 was compared with EnergyPlus, Ener-Win and measured data from a test cell and an occupied house. Chenath's results "compare favourably with the other two simulation engines and measured data with acceptable CV(RMSE)" (search extract) — [Adelaide repository](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/dspace/handle/2440/77158)
  - **Dewsbury** (University of Tasmania PhD and monograph; three test buildings in a cool temperate climate). "While the AccuRate software is well suited to modeling energy flows, there are discrepancies between the simulated and measured temperatures of the test buildings" (search extract) — [UTas](https://figshare.utas.edu.au/articles/thesis/The_empirical_validation_of_house_energy_rating_HER_software_for_lightweight_housing_in_cool_temperate_climates/23226212/1)
  - **Lomas et al. (1997)** (AIVC; historical). For the predecessor engine, Cheetah, "less than half the predictions ... were within the estimated error bands" (search extract) — [AIVC](https://www.aivc.org/sites/default/files/p_1068.pdf)
  - **Darwin field validation** (Williamson, University of Adelaide; CSIRO Darwin Living Lab, 2022–2025). It asks whether Chenath/AccuRate "can reliably predict the temperatures in a range of building types in Darwin" (search extract) — [2025 slides](https://research.csiro.au/darwinlivinglab/wp-content/uploads/sites/278/2025/10/2-Field-validaton-Uni-of-Adelaide-Williamson.pdf); [2022 paper](https://research.csiro.au/darwinlivinglab/wp-content/uploads/sites/278/2022/09/Darwin-field-validation-of-house-energy-rating-software.pdf)
    - Data: 20 naturally ventilated households (2013–14), and 30 houses plus 8 apartments (2020–21).
    - The findings were not retrieved.
- **Strength.** Moderate. The engine performs acceptably on loads; temperature prediction is weaker.
- **Counter-evidence.** None; this evidence mostly supports NatHERS.
- **H6V2 backfire.** High if Thermarate claims its software is "more accurate". No comparative evidence was found.
- **Marketing.** **Avoid** any "NatHERS software is inaccurate" claim.

### Inferences
**Issue scorecard (assessment):**

| # | Issue | Strength | H6V2 backfire | Marketing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | National meter-data validation still pending | Strong (that it is pending) | High | Avoid as an attack |
| 1.2 | Same rating, very different bills | Strong (individual homes) | High | Use with care (pathway-neutral) |
| 1.3 | Higher-rated temperate homes warmer in summer, more cooling | Moderate | Moderate–high | Use with care |
| 1.4 | Thermostat assumptions differ from behaviour (direction mixed) | Moderate–strong | High | Use with care (pathway-neutral) |
| 1.5 | Rebound | Weak for new homes | High | Avoid / use with care |
| 1.6 | Engine accuracy (supports NatHERS) | Moderate | High if claiming superiority | Avoid attacking |

- **No study found no relationship at all in a large sample.** The weak or no-correlation findings are small (5–10 homes) or old.
  - Examples: Williamson et al. 2010 (established); the older Adelaide paper; the Perth 10 homes.
  - The larger datasets find that ratings predict *heating* reasonably well on average and *cooling* poorly or perversely in temperate cities (O'Leary r² 0.32–0.55, established; CSIRO 2013; Willand 2016; CSIRO 209-household study).
- **The strongest accurate claim is about individual homes:** "the rating is not your bill".
- **The "performance gap" is mostly an occupant-and-assumption gap, not an engine gap** (1.4, 1.6). This makes it a weakness of simulation-based compliance generally, so it cuts against H6V2 as much as against NatHERS.
- **For inland WA:** the hot-dry Climate Zone 4 finding (lower trigger points, so more cooling than predicted) is the most recent field evidence on set-points. The study was in South Australia, and whether its results transfer to inland WA climate zones was not checked.
- **The CSIRO meter-data and apartment studies could land within the next year.** Any Thermarate message about the real-world performance of ratings should be reviewed when they are published.

### Gaps
- **CSIRO meter-data linkage:** no results, methods or timeline found.
- **CSIRO Apartment Energy Behaviour Study:** no first-year findings found.
- **CSIRO 209-household study:** the actual correlation statistics behind the "strong correlation" claim were not retrieved.
- **ACT data:** no ACT study linking Energy Efficiency Ratings (EER) to metered gas or electricity use was found, despite the ACT's long-standing EER disclosure.
- **Perth and WA:** no peer-reviewed WA study of measured energy against NatHERS ratings was found beyond the 2015 ten-home poster.
- **Gong (APVI 2018), "Does a 10 star rated house perform as designed":** only the title was found — [APVI](https://apvi.org.au/solar-research-conference/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Sihong-Gong-Does-a-10-star-rated-house-perform-as-designed.pdf)
- **Thermostat settings:** NatHERS's current climate-specific settings were not retrieved, so the size of the gaps in 1.4 cannot be stated.
- **Rebound:** no study of rebound specific to NatHERS-rated new homes was found.

## 2. Accuracy and consistency of ratings between assessors and tools

### Takeaway
**The only national test of assessor consistency is old.** In the 2013 NatHERS Benchmark Study, 344 assessors rated four plans. Reportedly only about one-third came within 0.25 stars of the correct rating, and most errors overstated efficiency. Nothing newer is public. Neither the quality-assurance regime nor the 2024 Compliance and Enforcement Framework publishes error statistics. A Performance Solution has less standardised quality assurance than NatHERS, so this is the line of attack most likely to backfire.

### Cited Findings

#### 2.1 Inter-assessor variability: the NatHERS Benchmark Study (2013)
- **What it is.** Different accredited assessors rating the same plans got materially different results.
- **Evidence.**
  - **Study design** (government; search extract) — [NatHERS Benchmark Study page](https://nathers.gov.au/node/88); [NatHERS Benchmark Study 2013](https://nathers.gov.au/node/87)
    - 344 volunteer assessors, May–December 2013, each modelling one of four randomly assigned plans in their chosen software.
    - The study was "undertaken to establish a baseline against which future improvements in the NatHERS scheme can be developed and evaluated".
  - **Results**, reported only in industry commentary. The search extract does not say which result it came from; it surfaced alongside BDAV's *"NatHERS mandatory accreditation makes sense"* — [BDAV, Issuu](https://issuu.com/bdav6/docs/165312_dm035_q3_intersect_hires/s/19568485)
    - "Of the 344 assessors that participated, only around one-third achieved a rating within 0.25 stars of the correct rating. Most of the errors overestimated the energy efficiency of the dwellings."
    - The study reportedly recommended changes to software, scheme management, assessor support, auditing and continuing professional development (CPD).
- **Strength.** Moderate. It was an official national study, but its results are known only second-hand, the primary report was not found, and it is 13 years old.
  - It predates later NatHERS Technical Notes, the 2016/2021/2024 accrediting-organisation protocols, mandatory accreditation and NCC 2022.
- **Counter-evidence.**
  - The study itself triggered reforms: a quality-assurance (QA) regime (2.2), accreditation and CPD.
  - No newer study shows that variability persists.
- **H6V2 backfire.** High. Performance Solutions have no national accreditation, benchmark study or QA audit regime for the modeller. In WA, approval rests with the registered building surveyor (strategy doc). A competitor can turn this argument around easily.
- **Marketing.** *Use with care*, for technical audiences only, with the 2013 date and the second-hand source stated. Better framed as a QA promise than as an attack:
  - "Any energy model depends on its inputs. We have every model independently checked before it goes to your surveyor."

#### 2.2 QA audits exist, but no error rates are published
- **What it is.** The Assessor Accrediting Organisations (AAOs) audit assessors, but no national error or non-compliance rates have been published.
- **Evidence.**
  - **AAO Protocol v2.0** (October 2021; government; search extract) — [AAO Protocol v2.0](https://www.homeenergyrating.gov.au/sites/default/files/2021-10/Assessor%20Accrediting%20Organisations%20Protocol%20v2.0%20_%20October%202021.pdf)
    - "Each year, AAOs will undertake a QA review of at least 20% of all Assessors it accredits ... based on a risk Assessment process."
    - AAOs report "a summary of errors found and actions taken" to the NatHERS Administrator.
  - **2016 protocol, July 2020 version** (search extract) — [AAO Protocol 2016](https://www.homeenergyrating.gov.au/sites/default/files/2020-07/AAO%20Protocol%202016%20-%20July%202020%20version.pdf)
    - "Where the Assessor receives a score of less than 80% from the QA review process, the Assessor will be subjected to Remedial Action."
    - Reviewers "score the accuracy of the Assessor's software inputs and processes".
  - **AAO Protocol v2.2** (dated November 2024, uploaded September 2025; search extract) — [AAO Protocol v2.2](https://www.homeenergyrating.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-09/AAOProtocol%20v2.2%2020250920.pdf)
    - The Administrator can compel an AAO to withdraw an assessor's accreditation.
    - AAOs must suspend assessors who fail QA and remedial action, pending investigation.
    - Cancellations must be notified within 5 business days; the earlier version allowed 10.
  - **Industry commentary on QA trends** (consultant/training firm; no figures; search extract). QA results show "a decline in alignment with the outcomes expected" — [Transformed, "Quality Assurance in the NatHERS industry"](https://www.transformed.com.au/nathers/quality-assurance-in-the-nathers-industry/)
  - **An assessor's account of a failed audit** (industry body newsletter; anecdote; search extract). The assessor scored 56%. - **Counter-evidence.** Building to the minimum is lawful compliance and says nothing about whether ratings are inaccurate.
- **H6V2 backfire.** High. Thermarate's own proposition is to "upgrade only what's needed" against the reference building (strategy doc). The ACL point applies to any energy-performance marketing, Thermarate's included.
- **Marketing.**
  - *Use* the minimum framing, which is already in the strategy doc: "7 stars is the minimum for the NatHERS route, not a premium."
  - **Avoid** criticising "building to the minimum".

## 3. The as-built gap: does what gets built match what was rated?

### Takeaway
**Ratings check the design, not the build.**

- **Inspections found real defects:**
  - NEEBP 2014–15: missing underfloor insulation, wrong roof colour, poor window sealing.
  - CSIRO 2015: about half of new homes above 15 ACH50, and one house with no ceiling insulation.
- **Airtightness has since improved substantially** (CSIRO 2024 testing), and the 2015 government pilot found only a small number of non-compliant homes.
- **No jurisdiction was found that verifies as-built energy performance of new houses.**
- **Regulators' audits are mostly desktop documentation reviews** with no published energy-specific results.

All of this applies equally to an H6V2 Performance Solution, so the opportunity for Thermarate is to offer verification, not to attack NatHERS.

### Cited Findings

#### 3.1 NEEBP Phase 1 (2014) and Phase 2 (2015)
- **What it is.** The National Energy Efficient Building Project (NEEBP), led by South Australia: a stakeholder study (Phase 1) followed by on-site inspection pilots (Phase 2).
- **Evidence.**
  - **Phase 1.** *Addition to the established item: primary sources and how it was received.*
    - NEEBP final report, November 2014 (government) — [NEEBP final report](https://architectureanddesign.com.au/getmedia/dfa17c83-a87b-4ae7-bc85-a17622fa965b/NEEBP-final-report-November-2014.aspx); [SA Government NEEBP page](https://energymining.sa.gov.au/industry/energy-efficiency-and-productivity/national-energy-efficiency-building-project)
    - ASBEC's headline: the report "highlights industry concerns with effectiveness of National Construction Code" — [ASBEC](https://www.asbec.asn.au/news-items/national-energy-efficient-building-report-highlights-industry-concerns-with-effectiveness-of-national-construction-code/)
    - Media headline: "Explosive report lifts the lid on Australia's building energy performance sham" — [Eco-Business](https://www.eco-business.com/ms/news/explosive-report-lifts-lid-australias-building-energy-performance-sham/)
  - **Phase 2 (2015)**, reported in early 2016 (professional and trade media summarising the SA Government results; search extract) — [The Fifth Estate](https://thefifthestate.com.au/?p=79646); [Australian Institute of Architects, EDG news Feb 2016](https://wp.architecture.com.au/news-media/edg-february-2016-news)
    - Scale: nine councils completed 86 inspections on 59 homes, at two build stages.
    - "Compliance varied across jurisdictions, however only a small amount of homes were deemed to be non-compliant."
    - Defects included:
      - wrong roof colour;
      - "insulation missing under flooring where specified";
      - inadequate window sealing;
      - lighting changed by homeowners after handover.
    - None of the participating councils collected all the energy-performance documents the NCC requires.
    - "an integrated approach to NCC compliance is needed, from design documentation through to approval and construction stages".
    - Next focus: design non-compliance, material substitution, and skills or knowledge gaps.
  - **Fifth Estate commentary** (search extract): a South Australian on-site pilot "found a gap between modeled and built energy features" — [The Fifth Estate, Spinifex column](https://thefifthestate.com.au/columns/spinifex/help-fix-the-ncc-hows-that-for-a-new-years-resolution/)
- **Strength.** Moderate. It was a government pilot, but the sample was small and the results are known through second-hand reporting. Phase 1 reflects perceptions; Phase 2 is observed.
- **Counter-evidence.** Phase 2 itself found only a small number of non-compliant homes. The Fifth Estate called the Phase 2 results less incendiary than Phase 1's.
- **H6V2 backfire.** High. The as-built gap does not depend on pathway. A Performance Solution depends on specific products and details, so substitution can invalidate it just as easily.
- **Marketing.** *Use with care.* State the small sample and the date:
  - "In a 2015 government pilot, inspectors found some new homes with missing underfloor insulation, the wrong roof colour or poor window seals. A rating checks the design, not the build."

#### 3.2 CSIRO House Energy Efficiency Inspections Project (2015) and airtightness studies 2015–2017
- **What it is.** CSIRO's field inspection of airtightness and insulation quality in recently built houses.
- **Evidence.** *Addition to the established 15.4 ACH50 average and variable insulation quality: primary source and detail.*
  - **The report.** Ambrose & Syme (CSIRO, December 2015, for the Department of Industry, Innovation and Science; government).
    - Scope: airtightness and installation quality of insulation and heating/cooling ductwork in "20 houses in each capital city", each up to three years old, using thermal imaging and visual inspection (search extract) — [NatHERS publication page](https://www.nathers.gov.au/publications/house-energy-efficiency-inspections-project); [report PDF (NatHERS)](https://www.nathers.gov.au/sites/default/files/House%2520Energy%2520Efficiency%2520Inspect%2520Proj.pdf); [report PDF (CSIRO)](https://research.csiro.au/energyrating/wp-content/uploads/sites/74/2016/05/House-Energy-Efficiency-Inspect-Proj.pdf)
  - **Coverage at the time** (2015/16; trade media; search extract) — [The Fifth Estate](https://thefifthestate.com.au/innovation/building-construction/push-for-air-tightness-standards-as-csiro-uncovers-poor-new-build-performance/)
    - "close to half of 129 new homes tested for air tightness across Australia's capital cities have returned poor results".
    - Roughly half exceeded 15 ACH50, "considered the upper limit for a newly constructed house in Australia".
    - Several exceeded 30 ACH50.
    - CSIRO recommended minimum airtightness standards in the NCC.
  - **Ambrose & Syme (2017), *Procedia Engineering*** (SBE16 Sydney; peer-reviewed conference; search extract) — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1877705817316697); [SBE16 PDF](https://www.sbe16sydney.be.unsw.edu.au/Proceedings/31864.pdf)
    - "well sealed houses are being constructed, but equally poorly performing houses are also still being built".
    - "one Brisbane based house was found to have no ceiling insulation installed".
    - Sixteen percent of ceiling spaces were not accessible, so they could not be inspected.
    - The search summary gives 125 blower-door tests here, against 129 in The Fifth Estate. The difference is unexplained and may reflect tests versus analysed homes.
- **Strength.** Strong for wide airtightness variability in 2015. Moderate for insulation defects: the findings are qualitative and no defect rate was retrieved.
- **Counter-evidence.** Airtightness improved markedly by 2024 (3.3).
- **H6V2 backfire.** High. Infiltration and insulation quality are equally unverified under either pathway.
- **Marketing.** *Use with care*, and always paired with the 2024 improvement (3.3). **Avoid** "new homes are leaky", which the strategy doc already flags.

#### 3.3 CSIRO air infiltration of new dwellings: 2024 testing, 2025 report
- **What it is.** CSIRO's repeat blower-door survey of recently built homes.
- **Evidence.** *Addition to the established "roughly matched NatHERS assumptions": sample, numbers and recommendations.*
  - **Sample and timing.** "233 newly built dwellings were tested in Melbourne, Sydney, Brisbane, Canberra and Adelaide, comprising of 105 apartments and 128 detached houses", all built in the previous four years. Results were publicised in August 2024; the report is hosted on the NatHERS site in an October 2025 folder (government; search extract) — [CSIRO report PDF](https://www.homeenergyrating.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-10/CSIRO%20Air%20Infiltration%20of%20New%20Dwellings%20in%20Australia%20Report.pdf); [NatHERS report page](https://www.nathers.gov.au/air-infiltration-report)
  - **Headline.** New homes were "up to 50 per cent more airtight than those tested in 2015". Leakage came mainly "from bathroom fans, sliding doors and poor or missing door seals" (search extract) — [CSIRO, Aug 2024](https://www.csiro.au/en/news/All/Articles/2024/August/testing-leakiness-australian-homes)
    - The project lead said "new Australian homes are performing better than ever before" (media; search extract) — [InDaily Qld / AAP, Aug 2024](https://www.indailyqld.com.au/news/archive/2024/08/13/feeling-those-winter-drafts-modern-homes-more-likely-keep-the-cold-air-out)
  - **Metrics** (second-hand). The report uses air permeability (q50, m³/h·m²), not ACH50. A blog summary gives a national mean of 6.86, with apartments at 5.8, single-storey houses at 6.8 and two-storey houses at 8.5 (search extract; check against the report) — [Hiperhaus summary](https://www.hiperhaus.com.au/learn/how-airtight-is-the-average-australian-home)
  - **The NatHERS assumption.** NatHERS "does not specifically define a level of air-tightness to be achieved" and uses a stack-and-wind infiltration formula. An earlier CSIRO-era estimate put the equivalent at about 15 ACH50 (search extract; the exact source among these results was not confirmed) — [SBE16 paper](https://www.sbe16sydney.be.unsw.edu.au/Proceedings/31864.pdf); [Hiperhaus](https://www.hiperhaus.com.au/learn/how-airtight-is-the-average-australian-home)
  - **Recommendations** (as reported in media; search extract) — [One Step Off The Grid](https://onestepoffthegrid.com.au/study-finds-newer-homes-more-airtight-but-efficiency-standards-still-need-tweaking/); [AAP](https://aapnews.com.au/news/feeling-the-cold-study-shows-newer-homes-are-more-cosy)
    - airtightness standards in the NCC;
    - controlled ventilation in apartments;
    - compulsory air barriers such as building wraps;
    - showing each home's airtightness value on energy-rating certificates.
- **Strength.** Strong. It is a large, recent field study by the scheme's own science agency.
- **Counter-evidence.** None on the direction of change. Note the coverage gap below.
- **WA relevance (inference).** Perth was not among the five cities, so WA construction types such as double brick are not represented in the 2024 data. The 2015 study covered every capital city.
- **H6V2 backfire.** High if airtightness is used to attack NatHERS: the evidence now favours new homes, and H6V2 assumes infiltration too.
  - Low if used as a service: Thermarate can offer a blower-door test and report the q50 value, which is what CSIRO recommends showing on certificates.
- **Marketing.** *Use*, as a service:
  - "CSIRO's 2024 tests found new homes far tighter than in 2015, but leakage still varies from house to house and is rarely measured. We'll test yours at handover."
  - **Avoid** "new homes are leakier than NatHERS assumes".

| 3.2 | 2015: half of homes above 15 ACH50; insulation defects | Strong (airtightness) / moderate (insulation) | High | Use with care, paired with 3.3 |
| 3.3 | 2024: up to 50% tighter; leakage still variable | Strong | High as attack / low as service | Use as service; avoid "leaky" |
| 3.4 | Substitution and undocumented changes | Moderate | High (higher for a Performance Solution) | Use with care (pathway-neutral) |
| 3.5 | Audits mostly desktop; no energy results published | Weak / moderate | Moderate–high in WA | Use with care |
| 3.6 | No as-built verification required | Moderate | High as attack / low as service | Use, as a service |

- **The as-built gap is real but pathway-neutral.** The only position that neither backfires nor misleads is positive: "we verify".
- **The evidence base is old.** The best quantified as-built evidence is from 2015. The 2024 airtightness study is the only recent quantified field data, and it shows improvement. Calling builds "worse" in 2026 is not supportable.
- **WA is under-represented.** It is missing from the 2024 airtightness sample and has no published audit results. Any WA-specific as-built claim lacks evidence, which is itself worth saying to surveyors.

### Gaps
- **HEEIP detail:** insulation and ductwork defect rates were not retrieved from the full 2015 report.
- **Victorian energy-efficiency audits:** results of the VBA's post-2011 energy-efficiency audit program, and any BPC energy-efficiency audit data, were not found.
- **NSW:** no Building Commission or NSW Planning audit of installed BASIX commitments was found.
- **WA:** no 2025–26 inspection results, and the 2026–27 priorities were not verified.
- **CSIRO 2024/25 report:** whether it says airtightness "roughly matched" NatHERS assumptions could not be verified. The q50 figures are second-hand.
- **NCC 2022 airtightness:** the exact clause for the optional envelope-sealing verification was not verified.
- **Performance Solutions:** no study of their as-built outcomes found.

## 4. Overheating: do higher-rated homes overheat?

### Takeaway
**A NatHERS star rating assumes air-conditioning is always available, so it does not measure free-running summer comfort or heat-stress resistance.**

- **Simulation** (UniSA / Hatvani-Kovacs) found higher stars did not reliably reduce cooling energy or overheating; some 6–8-star designs exceeded 35 °C.
- **Measured studies** found 6-star Melbourne homes slightly warmer than 4–5-star homes, and minimum-rated hot-dry South Australian homes overheating when air-conditioning was off.
- **Against very poor older stock,** higher ratings clearly reduce heat stress.
- **A national overheating method is being developed.**

H6V2 shares the same blind spot unless Thermarate adds a free-running check.

### Cited Findings

#### 4.1 The rating assumes heating and cooling are always available (CSIRO)
- **Evidence.**
  - "NatHERS star rating is based on the assumption that heating and cooling are always available in the house to keep the occupants thermally comfortable. This rating methodology can potentially deliver high star rated house overheated when in free-run operation without air conditioning." (CSIRO AHD, November 2019; government research agency; search extract) — [AHD, Nov 2019](https://ahd.csiro.au/?p=922)
  - CSIRO "simulated 28771 new dwelling designs without air conditioning" against the UK CIBSE TM59 overheating criteria, and is "collaborating with Australian research institutes in developing criteria for free-run residential buildings" (search extract) — [AHD, Nov 2019](https://ahd.csiro.au/?p=922)
  - **Regulatory direction.** A Residential Overheating Model for the national code uses an adaptive comfort model with degree-hours to quantify the "intensity & duration of exposure to heat stress". The search summary dates it 2026; date not confirmed (search extract) — [IEA EBC, "Code considerations on overheating – Australia"](https://www.iea-ebc.org/Data/publications/Code%20considerations%20on%20overheating%20-%20Australia.pdf)
- **Strength.** Strong. This is the scheme's science agency describing its own method.
- **Counter-evidence.** None on the method itself.
- **H6V2 backfire.** High. H6V2 compares conditioned heating and cooling loads with a reference building; my inference is that it carries the same blind spot. Low if Thermarate adds a voluntary free-running overheating check (for example TM59, or adaptive degree-hours).
- **Marketing.** *Use with care*:
  - "A star rating assumes the air-conditioner is available. It doesn't tell you how hot the house gets if it's off or the power's out, and we can check that too."
  - **Avoid** "NatHERS homes are dangerous in heatwaves".

#### 4.2 UniSA / Hatvani-Kovacs: NatHERS and heat-stress resistance (2016 report; 2018 *Energy and Buildings*)
- **Evidence.**
  - **2016 study** (University of South Australia; hosted by the ABCB; peer-reviewed version in 2018; simulation) — [ABCB-hosted PDF](https://www.abcb.gov.au/sites/default/files/resources/2022/Does-australian-nationwide-house-energy-rating-scheme-ensure-heat-stress-resistance.pdf); [CRC LCL PDF](https://www.lowcarbonlivingcrc.unsw.edu.au/sites/all/files/publications_file_attachments/hatvani-kovacs-2016-overheating_risk_in_the_australian_nationwide_house_energy_rating_scheme.pdf); [Adelaide record](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/e21afbc1-3c1f-4798-bb01-7a4400c3752a)
    - Design: six designs of a 211 m² single-storey home rated 6–8 stars, plus uninsulated double-brick and brick-veneer cases, simulated in AccuRate for Adelaide and Sydney heatwaves.
    - "a higher star rating does not necessarily coincide with a decrease in either cooling energy consumption, demand or overheating"; three homes rated 6, 7 and 8 stars exceeded 35 °C indoors (search extract).
  - **Media coverage** (search extract) — [ArchitectureAU](https://architectureau.com/articles/energy-rating-schemes-sabotaging-heat-stress-resistance-of-australian-homes-study-finds)
    - "In Adelaide, temperatures were higher in many [of the houses] with five stars or more than in a 2.3-star double-brick home during the investigated heatwave period".
    - The scheme "can even deliver buildings with worse heat resistance and increased reliance on air conditioning than traditional, energy-inefficient buildings".
  - **2018 *Energy and Buildings* paper**, "Heat stress-resistant building design in the Australian context" (peer-reviewed; per UniSA media release, 3 Jan 2018; search extract) — [UniSA release](https://unisa.edu.au/media-centre/Releases/2018/Over-reliance-on-air-conditioning-is-driving-up-power-prices-in-Australia)
    - "a newer home with a 6-star energy rating used the same amount of energy to cool the interior as an older double brick home with just 2.6 stars".
    - Recommends that "building codes need to incorporate separate energy ratings for heating and cooling" and integrate heat-stress resistance.
    - The 2.3-star and 2.6-star figures come from different outputs (indoor temperature vs cooling energy) and are not necessarily in conflict.
- **Strength.** Moderate. The work is peer-reviewed and hosted by the regulator's own body, but it is simulation-only, for one house type in two cities.
- **Counter-evidence.**
  - It is a simulation, not a measurement.
  - Higher ratings reduce heat stress compared with very poor stock (4.4).
  - Whether NCC load limits have since addressed this was not verified (see Gaps).
- **H6V2 backfire.** High. This is the same simulation logic, and double brick's advantage is a material effect, not a NatHERS-specific one.
- **WA relevance (inference).** Double brick is common in Perth, so a "your heavy-mass home may cope with heatwaves better than its stars suggest" message is plausible. It rests on Adelaide and Sydney simulations only.
- **Marketing.** *Use with care*:
  - "University of South Australia modelling found a higher star rating didn't always mean a cooler house in a heatwave. Ask for a summer comfort check."

#### 4.3 Measured summer evidence across climates
- **Evidence.**
  - **Melbourne (temperate):** 6-star homes were 0.89 °C warmer than 4–5-star homes at the 25 °C heatwave threshold (Willand, Ridley & Pears 2016; 1.3).
  - **Hot-dry South Australia** (2025, *Energy and Buildings*; peer-reviewed): "houses that have met the minimum star rating requirement according to NatHERS can still experience overheating when the air-conditioning is not in use" (search extract) — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0378778825014987)
  - **Humid subtropical** (2026, *Building and Environment*; peer-reviewed; 24 homes over a year): living rooms "accumulated substantially greater overheating than bedrooms (mean DhD 2,309°C·h versus 563°C·h)". The title flags "the limits of passive-design attribution" (search extract) — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0360132326007997)
  - **Darwin (tropical):** occupants were comfortable in warmer conditions than the codes assume (JCU 2022; 1.4), which points the other way.
- **Strength.** Moderate. Measured evidence across three climates shows overheating can occur in homes that meet or exceed the minimum. The studies do not isolate the rating as the cause.
- **Counter-evidence.**
  - Darwin's adaptive comfort.
  - Lochiel Park cooling matched predictions (1.2).
- **H6V2 backfire.** High; the same blind spot applies.
- **Marketing.** *Use with care*:
  - "Field studies in three Australian climates found homes meeting the star minimum can still overheat with the air-conditioning off."

#### 4.4 Counter-evidence: higher ratings cut heat stress compared with poor older stock
- **Evidence.**
  - Modelling of the Melbourne 2009 heatwave. This is a University of Newcastle repository record; authorship and journal were not confirmed in the extract (possibly Alam et al. 2016, *Sustainable Cities and Society*; verify) (search extract) — [University of Newcastle repository](https://nova.newcastle.edu.au/vital/access/%20/manager/Repository/uon:25224)
    - Residents of a 0.9-star house "were exposed to extreme heat stress conditions for almost 25 h compared to only 6 h experienced by the occupants of 5.4 star energy rated house".
    - Mortality "may reduce by 90% if entire existing lower energy star rated houses can be upgraded to minimum 5.4 star energy rating".
- **Strength.** Moderate. It is simulation, but the effect is large and plausible.
- **H6V2 backfire.** Not applicable. This finding supports efficient design under either pathway.
- **Marketing.** Supports *not* using "efficient homes overheat". *Use*:
  - "Efficient homes are far safer in heatwaves than old, uninsulated ones. The question is whether a design also stays comfortable without air-conditioning."

### Inferences
**Issue scorecard (assessment):**

| # | Issue | Strength | H6V2 backfire | Marketing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Rating assumes conditioning; free-running overheating not assessed | Strong | High (low if a check is added) | Use with care |
| 4.2 | UniSA: higher stars do not equal heat-resistance (simulation) | Moderate | High | Use with care |
| 4.3 | Measured overheating in minimum-or-better homes | Moderate | High | Use with care |
| 4.4 | Higher stars beat poor stock on heat stress | Moderate | Not applicable (supportive) | Use |

- **Overheating is the strongest real gap in what a star rating tells a buyer.** The scheme's own scientists acknowledge it (4.1), and a national method is in development. A voluntary free-running overheating check is therefore a credible, evidence-backed add-on.
- **It is not a reason to choose H6V2 over NatHERS:** neither assesses overheating by default. Marketing must say that Thermarate *adds* the check, not that the pathway includes one.
- **A reference-building comparison does not by itself ensure summer comfort.** If an H6V2 solution trades insulation or glazing against the reference, summer free-running performance could improve or worsen depending on the design. Only a separate overheating run can show which.

### Gaps
- **CSIRO's 28,771-design free-running study:** results not retrieved (what share of designs overheat, by star band and climate).
- **Current NCC heating and cooling load limits:** not checked here.
  - Whether NCC 2022/2025 adds an overheating check for housing was not verified.
  - Whether the separate heating and cooling load limits now partly answer the UniSA critique was also not verified.
- **Adelaide 7.5-star houses:** a search summary mentioned 7.5-star Adelaide houses exceeding 35 °C with the air-conditioning off, but its source could not be pinned down, so it is excluded.
- **Measured overheating in Perth or other WA houses:** none found.

## 5. What evidence supports NatHERS?

### Takeaway
**There is solid evidence that the scheme works on average.**

- Higher-rated homes use less heating energy.
- At Lochiel Park, high-rated homes used about 60% of the energy of decade-older homes.
- CSIRO's 2013 evaluation found a net 7% emissions cut.
- The engine passes benchmark validation.
- New homes are far more airtight than in 2015.
- NSW BASIX homes performed close to their estimates in aggregate.

Marketing must not contradict this. Much of it also supports simulation-based compliance in general, including H6V2.

### Cited Findings
- **Higher ratings mean less energy, especially for heating.** *Addition to O'Leary 2016 and CSIRO 2013.*
  - "Pursuing high NatHERS ratings results in major reductions in home energy use"; "the higher-rated Lochiel Park houses are on average using 60 per cent of the energy Mawson Lakes homes consume" (2016; trade media reporting a UniSA study; search extract) — [The Fifth Estate](https://thefifthestate.com.au/innovation/building-construction/high-nathers-ratings-deliver-results-so-long-as-builders-step-up/)
  - Homes "generally performed as the NatHERS ratings predicted, with a few exceptions" (search summary of the same study) — [The Fifth Estate](https://thefifthestate.com.au/?p=81654)
  - O'Leary argued the results put builders' performance in the spotlight and showed the need for stronger compliance checks — [The Fifth Estate](https://thefifthestate.com.au/innovation/building-construction/high-nathers-ratings-deliver-results-so-long-as-builders-step-up/)
  - *Strength:* strong for heating. *H6V2:* supportive of efficiency generally. *Marketing:* **use**, and do not deny it: "Higher-rated homes do use less heating energy on average. The question is the cheapest sound way to reach the Code's level for your design."
- **CSIRO 5-star evaluation (2013).** Higher-rated houses cut annual greenhouse emissions by 7% despite higher summer use (government; search extract) — [DCCEEW](https://www.dcceew.gov.au/sites/default/files/documents/evaluation-5-star-energy-efficiency-standard-residential-buildings-report.pdf)
  - *Strength:* strong (414 houses).
  - *Marketing:* *use with care*; cite it accurately with both the heating saving and the cooling increase.
- **Lochiel Park monitoring.** "Do the numbers stack up? Lessons from a zero carbon housing estate" (Berry et al. 2014; peer-reviewed; first two years of monitoring) found large cuts in operating energy, though few households reached net zero (search extract) — [Adelaide repository](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/ea2ffacb-8304-423f-978a-736590d3badb)
  - *Strength:* moderate.
- **CSIRO's 209-household study.** It reports a "strong correlation" between predicted and actual energy once occupants are accounted for. No statistics were seen (1.1).
  - *Strength:* weak to moderate until the figures are seen.
  - *Marketing:* **avoid** contradicting it.
- **Engine validation.**
  - BESTEST: "agrees very well with the reference programs" — [NatHERS](https://www.nathers.gov.au/sites/default/files/Validation%2520of%2520the%2520AccuRate%2520Simulation%2520Engine%2520Using%2520BESTEST_0.pdf)
  - Measured comparison: "acceptable CV(RMSE)" — [Adelaide](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/dspace/handle/2440/77158)
  - Details in 1.6. *Strength:* moderate. *H6V2:* supports simulation-based compliance in general, so it helps Thermarate's legitimacy as long as Thermarate doesn't attack it.
- **Airtightness improving.** New homes are "up to 50 per cent more airtight than those tested in 2015", and "new Australian homes are performing better than ever before" (2024; government and media; search extract) — [CSIRO, Aug 2024](https://www.csiro.au/en/news/All/Articles/2024/August/testing-leakiness-australian-homes); [InDaily Qld](https://www.indailyqld.com.au/news/archive/2024/08/13/feeling-those-winter-drafts-modern-homes-more-likely-keep-the-cold-air-out)
  - *Strength:* strong. *Marketing:* **avoid** "leaky new homes".
- **NSW BASIX validation.** Measured dwellings "performed well in reducing greenhouse gas emissions and were close to BASIX estimations" (search extract) — [GBPN listing](https://library.gbpn.org/resource/validating-and-improving-basix-energy-assessment-tool-low-carbon-dwellings-final-report)
  - *Strength:* moderate.
- **Heat stress.** Higher ratings sharply reduce heat-stress hours compared with very poor stock (4.4).
  - *Strength:* moderate (simulation).
- **Rebound need not erase savings.** The Victorian Healthy Homes trial found "no evidence of a rebound effect" alongside reduced gas use (retrofit; 1.5) — [SV](https://www.sustainability.vic.gov.au/research-data-and-insights/research/research-reports/the-victorian-healthy-homes-program-research-findings)
  - *Strength:* moderate. It covers retrofits, not new homes.
- **Older stock is much worse.** NSW homes built before 2004 average "just 2.1 stars", from research on almost 1,100 homes; these are modelled ratings (2024; media article by academics; search extract) — [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/living-in-a-glorified-tent-new-research-shows-huge-energy-gaps-between-older-and-newer-homes-286651)
  - Melbourne space-heating analysis: 4- and 5-star homes built after 2003 and 2006 used less heating energy per m² than older homes, from estimated loads (search extract) — [*Sustainability* (2012), "Does Energy Efficiency Reduce Emissions and Peak Demand? A Case Study of 50 Years of Space Heating in Melbourne"](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/4/7/1525)
  - *Strength:* moderate for the relative improvement; weak as measured evidence.
- **Predicted (not measured) Perth savings.** "a 7 star home in Perth is predicted to use 26% less energy for heating and cooling compared to a 6 star home"; an 8-star home "51% less" (government design guide; search extract) — [YourHome, Perth](https://www.yourhome.gov.au/node/86); [YourHome design options, Acacia Perth](https://www.yourhome.gov.au/sites/default/files/2021-08/Design%20Options%20-%20Acacia%20Perth.pdf)
  - *Strength:* modelled only. *Marketing:* useful when comparing pathways as long as it is labelled "predicted".
- **As-built defects were rarer than feared in the 2015 pilot.** NEEBP Phase 2 found "only a small amount of homes were deemed to be non-compliant" (3.1).
  - *Strength:* weak to moderate (small sample).

### Inferences
**These are cross-cutting conclusions for the report writer.**

1. **Most findings that look like criticisms of NatHERS are criticisms of simulation-based, design-stage compliance.** They are:
   - occupant variation;
   - thermostat assumptions;
   - conditioned-only comfort, which leaves free-running overheating unassessed;
   - the as-built gap;
   - unverified airtightness and insulation.

   Every one applies to an H6V2 Performance Solution. Only two NatHERS-specific weaknesses surfaced:
   - the 2013 assessor-consistency result;
   - possible quirks between tools.

   On both of those, H6V2 is *more* exposed, because it has no accreditation, QA sampling or shared engine.
2. **The safe, evidence-backed differentiators are additions Thermarate can make,** not flaws in NatHERS:
   - a blower-door test at handover (CSIRO recommends showing airtightness on certificates; 3.3, 3.6);
   - photo or thermal-imaging checks of insulation before lining (3.2, 3.4);
   - a free-running summer overheating check (4.1–4.3);
   - re-checking any substitution (3.4);
   - independent peer review of each model (2.1, 2.2).
3. **Claims to avoid**, because the evidence contradicts them or they would backfire:
   - "ratings bear no relation to energy use" (Section 5);
   - "new homes are leaky" (3.3);
   - "NatHERS software is inaccurate" (1.6);
   - "assessors get it wrong two-thirds of the time" (2013 data, second-hand, and a boomerang for H6V2);
   - "7-star homes overheat" (4.4; simulation-specific).
4. **The most accurate one-line summary** of the real-world evidence: "Higher-rated homes use less heating energy on average, but a rating predicts the building under standard assumptions, not your household's bills, your summer comfort with the air-conditioning off, or what was actually built."
5. **Timing risk.** CSIRO's meter-data linkage for 1.6 million ratings, and its apartment-monitoring findings, could change the evidence base within the next year. A strong positive correlation would undercut any messaging that implies ratings don't predict energy use.

### Gaps
- **CSIRO meter-data and apartment studies:** no published results by 22 October 2026 (1.1).
- **Primary documents not retrieved:** no full text could be opened (WebFetch was unavailable). All quotations need checking against the originals before publication, especially:
  - the 2013 Benchmark Study result;
  - the q50 airtightness figures;
  - the units of Willand et al.'s cooling-energy figure;
  - the 2025 hot-dry trigger-point values.
- **No evidence that H6V2 is more accurate:** no study was found comparing H6V2 Performance Solutions with measured performance, or comparing H6V2 outcomes with NatHERS outcomes for the same house. There is no evidence base for any claim that H6V2 is more accurate in the real world.
