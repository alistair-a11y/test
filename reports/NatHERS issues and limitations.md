# Most NatHERS flaws hit H6V2 too

The research documents 44 distinct issues with NatHERS (publicly branded Home Energy Rating since July 2026), but only **six give Thermarate a usable point of difference**, and each needs careful wording: 7 stars is the minimum for the NatHERS route, not a premium; in WA a NatHERS rating is judged on combined heating and cooling, while an H6V2 Performance Solution must match the reference house on each separately; steel-frame thermal bridging makes 7 stars harder for steel-framed homes; NatHERS scores a design against a fixed, floor-area-corrected star band, while H6V2 compares it with a Code-compliant version of the same house; the 0–10 star scale is easily misread; and 7-star upgrade costs vary widely by design. **Sixteen further issues are real and well evidenced but apply equally to H6V2**: no as-built check, no free-running overheating test, historical climate files, standard thermostat and occupancy settings, and per-square-metre scoring that hides total energy. H6V2 fixes the same assumptions in both of its models and is also a design-stage model, so these can only be used as education about both routes. **The other 22 should be avoided.** Attacks on assessor accuracy, software consistency, quality assurance, fees, or the link between ratings and real energy use rest on old, second-hand or contradicted evidence. They also hit H6V2 harder: it has no accreditation scheme, no quality-assurance sampling, no shared engine, no star label, and a history of misuse claims in WA before 2021. NatHERS's real blind spots become Thermarate's advantage only through services neither pathway requires: a blower-door and insulation check at handover, a re-check of product substitutions, a free-running overheating check, a future-climate run, independent review of every model, and an optional star rating for buyers and lenders. The research confirms the substance of the strategy's four-point fact-check but corrects or sharpens twelve facts in the strategy. They include the CSIRO 2013 result (less heating energy but more cooling energy), the 6.5- and 6-star minimums in northern WA, the low end of the published cost range, and the unverified claim that new-home certificates now read "Home Energy Rating (NatHERS)". **Every quotation in this report is a search-engine extract.** No source page could be opened during the research, so each quote must be checked on its live page before publication.

## Six of 44 documented issues give Thermarate an edge

The research comprised four note sets. They cover the rating method, real-world performance, governance and consumers, and policy and cost, and draw on about 148 web searches. Page fetches failed with DNS errors and proxy refusals, so **every quotation, figure and attribution below comes from search-engine extracts**. Some extracts paraphrase, and where an extract did not say which of two pages carried a passage, the attribution is marked approximate. Treat all of it as leads to verify, not as publishable copy.

The register turns on one fact: **H6V2 is not an escape from NatHERS's method. It is the same kind of method measured against a different benchmark.** H6V2(3) requires the proposed and reference models to use the same calculation method, the same floor plan including the size and location of glazing, and the same ventilation. They must also share the same zoning and hours of occupation, operating schedules (Table H6V2a), thermostat settings, occupancy, internal heat gains and air infiltration. H6V2(5) requires hourly, historical "typical year" weather, and both loads are annual totals calculated with heating and cooling available ([NCC 2022 Volume Two, Part H6](https://ncc.abcb.gov.au/editions/ncc-2022/adopted/volume-two/h-class-1-and-10-buildings/part-h6-energy-efficiency); [H6V2 review](../H6V2_shading_independent_review.md)). Any criticism about assumptions, weather data, what a rating leaves out, or the gap between design and build therefore lands on H6V2 as hard as on NatHERS.

Physics critiques transfer for a different reason than one research note assumed. H6V2 cannot use NatHERS software: it requires "a calculation method other than house energy rating software" that complies with ANSI/ASHRAE Standard 140 ([H6V2 review](../H6V2_shading_independent_review.md)). The note's inference that H6V2 shares the Chenath engine is therefore wrong. The critiques still transfer, because general-purpose engines differ from one another at least as much as NatHERS tools do. For uninsulated slabs, ground heat transfer differed by **around 50% or more between simulation tools** in CSIRO-authored benchmark work ([Chen, AIVC](https://www.aivc.org/sites/default/files/p_986.pdf)).

The pathways genuinely differ in three places.

- **The benchmark.** NatHERS uses a fixed star band for each of 69 climate zones, corrected for floor area. H6V2 uses the same house built to the Deemed-to-Satisfy (DTS) Provisions.
- **The test.** In WA, NatHERS applies one combined total. H6V2 tests heating and cooling separately in NCC climate zones 2–7.
- **The system.** NatHERS has accredited assessors and quality-assurance (QA) reviews covering at least 20% of assessors a year. It also has a dataset of **more than 1.6 million ratings** and a star label that lenders and disclosure schemes now use ([AAO Protocol v2.0](https://www.homeenergyrating.gov.au/sites/default/files/2021-10/Assessor%20Accrediting%20Organisations%20Protocol%20v2.0%20_%20October%202021.pdf); [CSIRO AHD, December 2025](https://ahd.csiro.au/nationwide-house-energy-rating-scheme-nathers-tools-update-december-2025/)). H6V2 has the building surveyor's review.

The first two are Thermarate's usable ground. The third is where Thermarate is exposed.

## The usable six turn on the benchmark, not the method

All six usable points are about **what a design is measured against**, not how the physics is modelled. That is why they survive the backfire test. Each still has a counter-argument, and two of them cut both ways.

- **U2 (WA's combined test).** H6V2's separate heating and cooling tests make it stricter than WA's combined NatHERS test. That is a credibility point, but it means a cooling-heavy design can reach 7 stars and still fail H6V2.
- **U4 (size correction).** NatHERS's floor-area correction marks large homes down and small homes up, so H6V2's same-house comparison is relatively kinder to large homes and harsher on small ones. That is a mechanism to explain to builders and surveyors, and it is exactly the kind of "big-house loophole" a critic would look for.

Both belong in the Pathway Check as screening questions: floor area, the balance of heating to cooling, and steel framing. Until priced jobs show which designs actually save, they are hypotheses, not claims.

**U3 (steel thermal bridging)** is the point Centurion Framing will most want to lead with, and the one that needs the most discipline. The evidence that bridging lowers steel-framed ratings is strong. It comes from CSIRO's modelling and NatHERS's own method, which benchmarks steel against timber and leaves timber-framed ratings "unaffected" ([NatHERS, Thermal bridging](https://www.nathers.gov.au/node/515)).

But the H6V2 reference house must itself comply with DTS Part 13.2, which carries its own thermal-break rules for steel. Nobody has yet shown that H6V2 narrows the steel gap.

Three rules therefore apply wherever U3 appears:

- Say "7 stars can be harder for steel", which is fair.
- Never say "NatHERS is unfair to steel" or "H6V2 avoids bridging". The first is contradicted by the ABCB's stated parity goal; the second invites a loophole story.
- Disclose the CFS partnership in every channel where this point runs.

## Sixteen real weaknesses are shared by every design-stage model

The best-evidenced criticisms of NatHERS are the least useful as weapons. That is because they describe **simulation-based, design-stage compliance in general**, and H6V2 is simulation-based, design-stage compliance. The scheme concedes many of them in its own words. CSIRO said a rating "measures the inherent thermal performance of the building shell all other things being equal" ([Productivity Commission submission, c. 2004–05](https://www.pc.gov.au/inquiries/completed/energy-efficiency/submissions/department_of_the_environment_and_heritage/sub069.pdf)). It later wrote that the rating "is based on the assumption that heating and cooling are always available" ([CSIRO AHD, 2019](https://ahd.csiro.au/?p=922)). The NT Government says "current energy ratings assess energy use when air conditioners are in use" ([NT Government](https://dipl.nt.gov.au/projects/darwin-living-lab-house-comfort-ratings)).

Those admissions are safe to quote. The strategy's "What's in a star?" series can carry them, provided each piece applies the same point to a Performance Solution or names what Thermarate adds. They can never be a reason to switch pathway.

The evidence also sets a ceiling on how hard these points can be pushed. On average, ratings work. Higher-star Adelaide homes used less heating and cooling energy, with **r² of 0.32 to 0.55** against metered use ([O'Leary et al., 2016](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0378778816301621)). Lochiel Park's higher-rated homes used about **60% of the energy** of decade-older homes ([The Fifth Estate](https://thefifthestate.com.au/innovation/building-construction/high-nathers-ratings-deliver-results-so-long-as-builders-step-up/)). The studies that found little or no relationship covered five to ten homes or are old.

The accurate one-line summary of the real-world evidence is therefore: **"Higher-rated homes use less heating energy on average, but a rating models the building under standard assumptions. It doesn't predict your household's bills, your summer comfort with the air-conditioner off, or what was actually built."** Seven of these issues are service hooks, marked (offer); the services section below turns them into products.

## Twenty-two lines fail on evidence, law or backfire

The avoid list fails in three ways.

**Some claims rest on old, second-hand or contradicted evidence.** The only national test of assessor consistency dates from 2013 and is known only through industry commentary. CSIRO's 2024 tests contradict "leaky new homes". The aggregate field studies contradict "ratings don't predict energy use". NatHERS's own area correction factor contradicts "bigger homes rate more easily".

**Some claims carry legal risk.** Claims about motive or conflicts, aimed at software vendors, accrediting organisations, CSIRO or assessors, invite defamation and injurious-falsehood claims. Fear claims and unsupported comparisons engage the Australian Consumer Law, as the strategy's guardrails already note.

**Most of them backfire, because the two pathways are not symmetrical.** NatHERS has a system: accredited assessors, quality-assurance (QA) sampling of at least 20% of assessors a year, a public register, one shared engine, and more than 1.6 million certificates in a national dataset. An H6V2 Performance Solution has a report and a building surveyor. Every attack on NatHERS's quality control therefore invites the reply "and who checks yours?"

WA gives that reply a local edge. In The Fifth Estate, WA architect Sid Thoo made three claims (undated; probably 2019–21):

- He described "the misuse of the alternative solution … called the verification using a reference building (VURB) method" as "unfortunately common".
- He reported "a glut of homes with an equivalent energy rating as low as two stars".
- Citing CSIRO data, he estimated that "around 80 per cent of homes in 2016-2018 built in WA" went through VURB or non-accredited assessors ([The Fifth Estate](https://thefifthestate.com.au/innovation/residential-2/are-new-homes-in-western-australia-about-to-get-a-whole-lot-more-energy-efficient/)).

HIA reports that NatHERS-based software stopped being permitted for VURB in WA only when NCC 2019 Amendment 1 was adopted there, on **1 May 2021** ([HIA](https://hia.com.au/resources-and-advice/sustainable-homes/energy-efficiency/articles/applying-ncc-2019-energy-efficiency-wa)). The ABCB handbook uses "VURB" for H6V2 itself ([H6V2 review](../H6V2_shading_independent_review.md), handbook pp 93 and 113). That makes this criticism of Thermarate's own route, not of NatHERS.

The data are from 2016–18 and the 80% figure is one practitioner's estimate, but WA surveyors and assessors will remember the story. The campaign needs a prepared answer setting out what the method requires today:

- the reference house must comply with the DTS Provisions of Parts 13.2, 13.3 and 13.5;
- NatHERS software is barred (in WA since 1 May 2021), and an ASHRAE 140 tool is required;
- heating and cooling schedules, thermostat settings and occupancy are prescribed (NCC 2022);
- heating and cooling are tested separately in climate zones 2–7;
- the design brief is agreed with the surveyor before modelling.

Behind that answer sits Thermarate's own published method.

## Seven services turn shared blind spots into Thermarate's edge

The most useful finding for the campaign is a gap. What a star rating cannot tell a buyer is well evidenced and acknowledged by the scheme itself, and neither pathway fills it. A firm that fills it voluntarily earns a point of difference without attacking anyone.

Three of these gaps are also where NatHERS itself is heading:

- **Airtightness.** CSIRO recommends showing airtightness on certificates, and AccuRate's regulatory mode already displays it ([NatHERS air infiltration report](https://nathers.gov.au/air-infiltration-report); [NatHERS News](https://nathers.gov.au/node/596)).
- **Overheating.** A national residential overheating method is in development ([IEA EBC](https://www.iea-ebc.org/Data/publications/Code%20considerations%20on%20overheating%20-%20Australia.pdf)).
- **Disclosure.** Star disclosure is spreading through lenders and state schemes ([pv magazine](https://www.pv-magazine-australia.com/?p=17014)).

If those reforms reach NatHERS certificates first, an H6V2 report that says nothing on airtightness, summer comfort or stars will look less transparent than the route it competes with. Offering these services now gets ahead of that.

Every service below is something **Thermarate adds**. None is part of H6V2. The wording must keep that clear, and must not promise a performance outcome, because a promise about future performance needs reasonable grounds under ACL s4.

The services also start to build the evidence the campaign lacks. **No study of H6V2 Performance Solutions' savings, built performance or consistency between modellers was found.** Blower-door results and overheating checks on Thermarate's own jobs would be the first such data. They are publishable only with permission, and with the sample size stated.

The same register sharpens the free **Pathway Check**. Floor area (U4), steel framing (U3) and the heating-to-cooling balance (U2) are screening questions, and each needs testing on priced jobs:

- **Large homes are the likeliest H6V2 wins,** because NatHERS marks them down. **Steel-framed designs are worth testing early:** NatHERS's bridging penalty is real, but the H6V2 reference also carries DTS thermal-break rules for steel.
- **Small homes and cooling-heavy designs are likelier to stay with NatHERS.** H6V2 removes NatHERS's uplift for small homes, and it tests cooling separately, which a cooling-heavy design may fail.

## Conclusion

The register shows an asymmetry that should shape the campaign. NatHERS's weaknesses are mostly shared with H6V2, while H6V2's distinctive weaknesses are its own: no QA system, no star, no national dataset, and a history of misuse claims in WA. The campaign's credibility will therefore depend less on what it says about NatHERS than on whether Thermarate privately builds the system NatHERS has publicly. That means a published method, independent review of every model, verification at handover, and an honest record of the jobs where NatHERS was cheaper. In practice, every "What's in a star?" post should end on what Thermarate adds, not on what NatHERS lacks.

The evidence is also moving, and every move makes attacking NatHERS riskier and verifying and disclosing more valuable:

- CSIRO's link between its 1.6 million ratings and meter data, and its apartment monitoring, could publish within a year.
- Airtightness is heading onto certificates.
- A national overheating method is in development.
- Star disclosure is spreading through lenders and state schemes.
- The Opposition's proposal has put the standard itself in political play.

Review this register when CSIRO's meter-data results appear, and again before Gate B and the February 2027 public launch. Above all, check every quotation and figure on its live page before it is published: the whole register was built from search-engine extracts, because no source page could be opened.
